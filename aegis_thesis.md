# ABSTRACT

The rapid proliferation of artificial intelligence systems across critical sectors (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, 2019) has created an urgent demand for robust governance frameworks capable of ensuring regulatory compliance, managing systemic risk, and preserving data sovereignty. Existing commercial AI governance platforms are predominantly proprietary, cloud-hosted Software-as-a-Service solutions that impose significant vendor lock-in, restrict on-premise deployment, and remain financially inaccessible to small and medium enterprises operating under strict data residency requirements. This thesis presents Aegis.AI, a full-stack, self-hostable Artificial Intelligence Governance, Risk, and Compliance platform designed to address these deficiencies. The system implements a multi-tenant, schema-isolated PostgreSQL architecture and supports continuous compliance tracking across four major regulatory frameworks: the European Union AI Act, ISO/IEC 42001 (Artificial Intelligence Management Systems), ISO/IEC 27001 (Information Security Management Systems), and the NIST Artificial Intelligence Risk Management Framework 1.0. Aegis.AI integrates a quantitative risk scoring algorithm, an automated Large Language Model evaluation pipeline powered by the DeepEval framework, a real-time audit event streaming system utilizing Redis Pub/Sub and Server-Sent Events, and a headless document rendering engine for generating audit-grade compliance reports. The platform was developed using a PERN stack augmented with a Python FastAPI evaluation microservice, containerized via Docker Compose and Kubernetes, and delivered across twelve iterative development sprints. System evaluation demonstrated API response latencies below 50 milliseconds, real-time notification delivery within 100 milliseconds, and an overall system accuracy of 99%. The platform's open architecture enables organizations to implement AI governance as a continuous, measurable discipline rather than a periodic compliance exercise, thereby advancing the state of practice in responsible AI deployment.

*Keywords:* artificial intelligence governance, regulatory compliance, EU AI Act, ISO 42001, NIST AI RMF, risk management, multi-tenancy, large language model evaluation, self-hosted platform

# Chapter 1

# Introduction

## 1.1 Background and Motivation

The integration of artificial intelligence into organizational workflows has accelerated at an unprecedented pace over the past decade. From automated credit scoring and predictive maintenance to natural language processing-based customer service and AI-assisted medical diagnostics, machine learning systems now influence decisions that directly affect individuals' livelihoods, safety, and fundamental rights. According to the International Data Corporation (2024), global spending on AI systems surpassed USD 154 billion in 2023 and is projected to exceed USD 300 billion by 2026, reflecting the extraordinary momentum behind enterprise AI adoption across virtually every industry sector.

This rapid proliferation, however, has exposed a critical and widening gap between the deployment velocity of AI systems and the institutional capacity to govern them responsibly. High-profile failures, from algorithmic hiring tools exhibiting gender bias to autonomous vehicle systems making catastrophic decisions, have demonstrated that poorly governed AI carries systemic risks that extend far beyond individual product failures. These incidents have catalyzed a global regulatory response of historic proportions. The European Union's Artificial Intelligence Act (EU AI Act), adopted in 2024, constitutes the world's first comprehensive binding regulatory framework for AI, establishing risk-based obligations across the entire AI lifecycle. Simultaneously, standards bodies including the International Organization for Standardization and the National Institute of Standards and Technology have published frameworks, ISO/IEC 42001 and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0, respectively, that provide complementary, process-oriented guidance for institutionalizing AI governance within organizations.

For organizations operating AI systems, compliance with these frameworks is no longer optional. Article 9 of the EU AI Act mandates that providers of high-risk AI systems establish and maintain a comprehensive risk management system that is "a continuous iterative process run throughout the entire lifecycle." Article 72 further requires ongoing post-market monitoring of deployed systems, with documented obligations for incident reporting and corrective action. Failure to comply carries penalties of up to EUR 30 million or 6% of global annual turnover, figures that demand serious institutional attention.

Yet despite this urgent regulatory landscape, the tooling available to organizations seeking to implement AI governance remains deeply inadequate. The market is dominated by proprietary, cloud-hosted Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) platforms, such as Credo AI, OneTrust AI Governance, and IBM OpenPages, that, while feature-rich, impose significant limitations. These platforms store sensitive governance artifacts, compliance evidence, and model documentation on third-party infrastructure, creating data sovereignty concerns that are incompatible with the security policies of many enterprises, government agencies, and regulated industries. They operate on subscription pricing models that render them prohibitively expensive for small and medium-sized enterprises. They lack support for self-hosted, on-premise, or private cloud deployments. Furthermore, they typically treat governance as a static audit function rather than a continuous, real-time operational discipline integrated into the AI development lifecycle.

This creates a fundamental dilemma for organizations that must demonstrate compliance with the EU AI Act and aligned standards: they either accept the data sovereignty and cost trade-offs of commercial SaaS platforms, or they attempt to construct piecemeal governance processes from general-purpose project management and document management tools that were never designed for AI governance workflows. Neither approach is satisfactory from a security, compliance, or operational efficiency standpoint.

Aegis.AI was conceived and developed in direct response to this gap. The platform is a full-stack, self-hostable AI Governance, Risk, and Compliance (GRC) system that enables organizations to manage the complete AI lifecycle, from model registration and risk assessment through continuous compliance monitoring, LLM evaluation, post-market surveillance, and audit reporting, on infrastructure entirely under their own control. By making the platform deployable on-premise or within a private cloud environment, Aegis.AI preserves data sovereignty without sacrificing the functional sophistication required for genuine regulatory compliance.

## 1.2 Problem Statement

The current landscape of AI governance tooling fails to serve the full spectrum of organizations required to comply with emerging AI regulations in at least four critical dimensions.

**First, the data sovereignty problem.** The EU AI Act and aligned standards require organizations to maintain extensive, sensitive documentation, including model cards, training data provenance records, bias assessment results, incident logs, and internal audit trails. When these artifacts are stored on third-party SaaS infrastructure, organizations lose effective control over their most sensitive compliance data. For regulated industries such as financial services, healthcare, and defense, this loss of sovereignty is not merely a preference but a regulatory prohibition. Existing commercial platforms provide no viable path to resolving this tension.

**Second, the cost accessibility problem.** Enterprise AI governance platforms typically operate on pricing models that assume large-enterprise customers with substantial IT budgets. Annual licensing costs for platforms such as Credo AI or OneTrust AI Governance routinely exceed USD 50,000-100,000 per year for meaningful feature access, placing comprehensive governance tooling effectively out of reach for the majority of the global market, including small and medium enterprises, academic institutions, non-governmental organizations, and public-sector bodies in developing economies that are nonetheless subject to regulatory obligations.

**Third, the compliance fragmentation problem.** Organizations operating globally must simultaneously satisfy multiple, partially overlapping regulatory frameworks, the EU AI Act, ISO/IEC 42001, ISO/IEC 27001, and the NIST AI RMF, each with distinct control structures, lifecycle requirements, and evidence standards. No single open-source or affordable tool currently provides integrated, cross-framework compliance management. Organizations are therefore forced to manage compliance across multiple disconnected systems, creating substantial operational overhead and increasing the risk of compliance gaps.

**Fourth, the static governance problem.** AI governance is inherently dynamic: model behavior drifts, regulatory requirements evolve, organizational risk profiles change, and new threats emerge continuously. Yet existing governance approaches, whether conducted on spreadsheets, document management systems, or even commercial GRC platforms, treat compliance as a periodic, point-in-time activity rather than a continuous operational discipline. The EU AI Act's explicit requirement for continuous risk management systems and real-time post-market monitoring cannot be satisfied by tools that produce static compliance snapshots.

Aegis.AI addresses all four dimensions simultaneously: it is self-hostable (resolving the data sovereignty problem), openly licensed under BSL 1.1 (addressing cost accessibility for non-commercial use), multi-framework in its compliance coverage (solving compliance fragmentation), and architecturally designed around real-time, continuous governance processes (overcoming static governance limitations).

## 1.3 Research Questions

This thesis investigates the following primary and secondary research questions:

**Primary Research Question:** How can a scalable, privacy-preserving, and self-hostable software architecture be designed to support continuous AI governance, multi-framework regulatory compliance management, and quantitative AI risk assessment across an organization's full AI model portfolio?

**Secondary Research Questions:**

1. What architectural patterns are required to achieve complete data isolation between multiple organizational tenants while maintaining operational efficiency in a shared infrastructure environment?
2. How can quantitative risk scoring algorithms be designed to provide objective, reproducible risk assessments for AI models, vendors, and projects within a governance platform?
3. What technical mechanisms are required to enable real-time compliance event monitoring and audit trail generation in a production AI governance platform?
4. How can Large Language Model evaluation, including assessment of hallucination, bias, correctness, and faithfulness, be integrated into an organizational AI governance workflow?
5. What performance characteristics must an AI governance platform exhibit to support production enterprise use, and can these be achieved in a self-hosted architecture?

## 1.4 Objectives

The specific objectives of this research are as follows:

1. To design and implement a multi-tenant, schema-isolated database architecture capable of supporting complete data sovereignty for multiple independent organizations within a shared Aegis.AI deployment.
2. To develop a comprehensive compliance tracking engine covering the EU AI Act (13 control categories, approximately 60 controls), ISO/IEC 42001 (Clauses 4-10, Annex A and B), ISO/IEC 27001 (7 clauses, 93 Annex A controls), and the NIST AI RMF 1.0 (GOVERN, MAP, MEASURE, MANAGE functions, approximately 70 subcategories).
3. To design and validate a quantitative risk scoring algorithm that produces objective, reproducible risk scores for AI-related risks based on independently assessed likelihood and severity dimensions.
4. To implement a fully automated LLM evaluation pipeline using the DeepEval framework, capable of assessing model outputs on correctness, faithfulness, hallucination rate, and bias/fairness dimensions.
5. To architect and implement a real-time audit event streaming system using Redis Pub/Sub and Server-Sent Events, enabling continuous monitoring of governance activities.
6. To validate the platform's performance characteristics through systematic benchmarking and demonstrate that a self-hosted architecture can meet enterprise production requirements.

## 1.5 Scope and Delimitations

Aegis.AI is scoped as an AI governance platform targeted at organizations that deploy, develop, or procure AI systems subject to regulatory oversight. The platform provides governance management infrastructure but does not itself make regulatory determinations, users and their legal/compliance teams retain responsibility for interpreting regulatory requirements and determining the adequacy of their compliance posture. The LLM evaluation module supports evaluation of models accessible via API (OpenAI, Anthropic, OpenRouter) and does not currently support evaluation of locally-hosted models beyond those accessible through OpenRouter's routing infrastructure. The platform's compliance framework coverage is calibrated to the versions of the EU AI Act, ISO/IEC 42001, ISO/IEC 27001, and NIST AI RMF 1.0 current as of the 2025 implementation date and does not automatically update to reflect regulatory amendments.

## 1.6 Significance of the Study

This research makes several contributions to the fields of AI governance, software engineering, and regulatory compliance technology. Academically, it advances the literature on AI governance system design by providing a detailed, empirically validated architectural reference for a comprehensive, multi-framework AI GRC platform, a class of system for which published design literature remains sparse. It contributes a formal articulation of the schema-per-tenant multi-tenancy pattern as applied specifically to the data isolation requirements of AI governance contexts.

Practically, Aegis.AI demonstrates that comprehensive AI governance tooling need not be the exclusive domain of well-funded enterprises. The platform's self-hostable, openly licensed architecture makes production-grade governance tooling accessible to organizations that have been underserved by the commercial market. This has particular significance for organizations in developing economies, public sector bodies, academic institutions, and civil society organizations that must nonetheless comply with AI regulations when deploying AI systems affecting EU-resident individuals.

From a regulatory standpoint, the platform's architecture provides a concrete implementation reference for the "continuous risk management system" and "post-market monitoring" requirements mandated by the EU AI Act, contributing to the corpus of technical knowledge available to standard-setters and regulators seeking to understand the practical implementation implications of regulatory obligations.

# Chapter 2

# Literature Review

## 2.1 Artificial Intelligence Regulation: An Emerging Imperative

The governance of artificial intelligence systems has emerged as one of the most consequential policy challenges (Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development, 2019; United Nations Educational, Scientific and Cultural Organization, 2021) of the early twenty-first century. Scholars, policymakers, and technologists have increasingly converged on the view that the development of AI without appropriate governance structures poses risks not merely to individual consumers but to democratic institutions, public safety, and fundamental human rights (Dafoe, 2018; Floridi et al., 2019; Jobin et al., 2019). This recognition has driven a substantial and growing body of regulatory activity across jurisdictions worldwide.

Early governance approaches were predominantly voluntary, taking the form of ethical principles documents, the European Commission's early policy explorations (European Commission, 2020), industry codes of conduct, and guidance publications from national AI strategy bodies. Floridi et al. (2019) conducted a systematic analysis of 84 AI ethics guidelines published between 2016 and 2019 and identified a convergence around five core principles: beneficence, non-maleficence, autonomy, justice, and explicability. While this convergence was intellectually significant, critics noted that voluntary principles without enforcement mechanisms produced little observable change in development practice (Jobin et al., 2019; Hagendorff, 2020). Hagendorff (2020) analyzed 22 AI ethics guidelines and found that the majority failed to address the structural organizational and economic incentives that produce harmful AI systems, focusing instead on aspirational behavioral guidance that practitioners were unlikely to internalize.

The inadequacy of voluntary approaches has driven a transition toward binding regulation in multiple jurisdictions. In the European Union, this culminated in the adoption of the EU AI Act in 2024, the world's first comprehensive binding legal framework for AI. In the United States, Executive Order 14110 on Safe, Secure, and Trustworthy Artificial Intelligence (The White House, 2023) directed federal agencies to develop AI risk management practices aligned with NIST frameworks. China has enacted regulations specifically targeting recommendation algorithms, generative AI, and deep synthesis technologies. The United Kingdom has adopted a "pro-innovation" sector-specific regulatory approach while the AI Safety Institute conducts evaluations of frontier models. This global regulatory fragmentation creates significant compliance complexity for multinational organizations, complexity that governance platforms must be capable of managing (Cihon, 2019).

## 2.2 The European Union AI Act

The EU AI Act (Regulation (EU) 2024/1689) represents the most comprehensive and consequential AI regulatory instrument (Veale & Borgesius, 2021) currently in force globally, originating from the initial legislative proposal tabled by the European Commission (2021). Its risk-based architecture classifies AI systems into four categories, unacceptable risk, high risk, limited risk, and minimal risk, and assigns proportionate regulatory obligations to each (European Parliament and Council of the European Union, 2024).

**Unacceptable Risk Systems** are prohibited outright. These include AI systems that deploy subliminal or manipulative techniques to distort behavior, systems that exploit vulnerabilities of specific groups, and biometric categorization systems that infer sensitive attributes from physical characteristics. Social scoring systems operated by public authorities and real-time remote biometric identification systems in publicly accessible spaces are similarly prohibited with narrow exceptions.

**High-Risk AI Systems** bear the most substantial compliance burden under the Act. Article 6 defines two categories of high-risk systems: AI systems that constitute safety components of products covered by Union harmonization legislation (such as medical devices, machinery, and aviation equipment), and AI systems deployed in eight specific high-risk application areas listed in Annex III, including biometric identification, critical infrastructure management, education, employment, access to essential services, law enforcement, migration and asylum processing, and administration of justice.

The obligations imposed on providers of high-risk AI systems are extensive and operationally demanding. Article 9 mandates a continuous, documented risk management system covering identification and analysis of known and foreseeable risks, estimation and evaluation of risks arising from intended use and reasonably foreseeable misuse, and evaluation of risks arising from post-market monitoring data. Crucially, Article 9(4) specifies that the risk management system must be a "continuous iterative process run throughout the entire lifecycle", a requirement that fundamentally cannot be satisfied by periodic compliance audits. Article 10 imposes data governance requirements, including documentation of training, validation, and testing datasets. Article 11 mandates technical documentation. Article 12 requires automatic logging of operations throughout the AI system's lifecycle. Article 13 mandates transparency and provision of instructions for use. Articles 14 and 15 require human oversight mechanisms and robustness, accuracy, and cybersecurity measures, respectively.

Article 72 establishes the post-market monitoring obligation, requiring providers to actively collect and review data on the performance of high-risk AI systems throughout their operational lifetime, proactively identifying opportunities for corrective action and reporting serious incidents to market surveillance authorities within defined timeframes.

The penalties for non-compliance are substantial. Article 99 of the AI Act provides for administrative fines of up to EUR 35 million or 7% of total worldwide annual turnover for violations related to prohibited AI practices, EUR 15 million or 3% for violations of other provisions of the Regulation, and EUR 7.5 million or 1.5% for the supply of incorrect information to notified bodies or national competent authorities.

As summarized in Table 2.1, the EU AI Act establishes a hierarchical risk taxonomy comprising Unacceptable Risk, High Risk, Limited Risk, and Minimal Risk systems, mapping each category to distinct legal compliance and conformity assessment obligations.

## 2.3 ISO/IEC 42001: Artificial Intelligence Management Systems

ISO/IEC 42001:2023 specifies requirements for an Artificial Intelligence Management System (AIMS), a systematic organizational framework for managing AI-related risks and responsibilities across the organization's AI activities (International Organization for Standardization, 2023a). The standard is structured around seven clauses (Clauses 4-10) following the Annex SL high-level structure common to all ISO management system standards, facilitating integration with ISO/IEC 27001 and ISO 9001.

Clause 4 addresses organizational context, requiring organizations to identify internal and external factors affecting AI management, understand interested parties' needs and expectations, and define the scope of the AIMS. Clause 5 covers leadership, requiring top management commitment, establishment of an AI policy, and assignment of AIMS-related roles and responsibilities. Clause 6 addresses planning, including AI risk and impact assessment processes. Clause 7 covers support requirements including resources, competence, awareness, communication, and documented information. Clause 8 specifies operational planning and control. Clause 9 addresses performance evaluation, including monitoring, measurement, internal audit, and management review. Clause 10 covers improvement, including nonconformity management and continual improvement.

Annex A of the standard (International Organization for Standardization, 2023b) provides a catalogue of controls addressing specific aspects of AI system development and deployment, covering data management, AI system lifecycle, AI transparency, AI system impact assessment, and AI objectives setting. Annex B provides guidance on implementing the Annex A controls.

For organizations subject to the EU AI Act, ISO/IEC 42001 certification provides a recognized mechanism for demonstrating organizational competence in AI governance, though it does not constitute legal compliance with the Act. The relationship between the two frameworks is complementary: the EU AI Act specifies legal requirements at the system level, while ISO/IEC 42001 provides an organizational management system standard that supports consistent implementation of those requirements across a portfolio of AI systems.

## 2.4 ISO/IEC 27001: Information Security Management Systems

ISO/IEC 27001:2022 specifies requirements for establishing (International Organization for Standardization, 2022), implementing, maintaining, and continually improving an Information Security Management System (ISMS). Its relevance to AI governance derives from the fact that AI systems are inherently information-intensive, they depend on large datasets, generate extensive logs, and often process sensitive personal data, making information security controls an integral component of comprehensive AI governance (International Organization for Standardization, 2022).

The 2022 revision reorganized Annex A from 114 controls across 14 domains to 93 controls across 4 themes: organizational controls (37 controls), people controls (8 controls), physical controls (14 controls), and technological controls (34 controls). New controls added in the 2022 revision include threat intelligence, information security for use of cloud services, configuration management, data masking, data leakage prevention, monitoring activities, and secure coding, several of which are directly applicable to AI system governance.

The integration of ISO/IEC 27001 within an AI governance platform is synergistic rather than merely additive. AI governance requires evidence management (addressed by ISO/IEC 27001 Clause 7.5), access control (Annex A Organizational Controls 5.15-5.18), incident management (Annex A Organizational Controls 5.24-5.28), and supplier relationship management (Annex A Organizational Controls 5.19-5.22, which maps directly to AI vendor risk management). Aegis.AI implements ISO/IEC 27001 tracking natively within the same platform as AI-specific frameworks, enabling organizations to manage both domains from a single interface.

## 2.5 NIST AI Risk Management Framework 1.0

The National Institute of Standards and Technology AI Risk Management Framework (NIST AI RMF 1.0), published in January 2023, provides voluntary guidance for organizations seeking to manage risks associated with AI system design, development, deployment, and evaluation (National Institute of Standards and Technology, 2023). Unlike the EU AI Act's binding legal obligations, the NIST AI RMF operates as a flexible, sector-agnostic framework designed for integration into existing risk management practices.

The NIST AI RMF is organized around four core functions:

**GOVERN** establishes the organizational policies, processes, and cultural practices necessary for effective AI risk management. It addresses organizational accountability, workforce training, risk tolerance thresholds, and the integration of AI risk management into enterprise risk management. The GOVERN function contains six categories and approximately 16 subcategories.

**MAP** identifies and frames the context for AI risk assessment. It covers the characterization of AI system purpose and intended use context, identification of stakeholders and impacted populations, and systematic cataloguing of potential risks. The MAP function contains five categories and approximately 23 subcategories.

**MEASURE** provides for the analysis and assessment of identified risks using both qualitative and quantitative methods. It covers the analysis of AI risk metrics, evaluation of risk treatments, and assessment of residual risk. The MEASURE function contains four categories and approximately 14 subcategories.

**MANAGE** addresses the prioritization and treatment of identified risks through risk mitigation plans, residual risk acceptance processes, incident response procedures, and organizational learning from AI system performance. The MANAGE function contains four categories and approximately 16 subcategories.

## 2.7 Algorithmic Accountability and Explainability

The governance of AI systems is inseparable from questions of algorithmic accountability, the attribution of responsibility for AI system decisions and outcomes to identifiable human actors and organizational structures (Diakopoulos, 2016; Wieringa, 2020; Mittelstadt et al., 2016; Wieringa, 2020). Bovens (2007) defines accountability as a relationship between an actor and a forum in which the actor has an obligation to explain and justify conduct, and the forum can pose questions and pass judgment. Translating this concept to AI governance requires platforms capable of maintaining complete, immutable audit trails of governance decisions, risk assessments, and compliance determinations, enabling accountability to both internal governance bodies and external regulators.

Diakopoulos (2016) identifies four dimensions of algorithmic accountability relevant to governance system design: (1) the ability to audit the algorithm itself; (2) the transparency of input data and training procedures; (3) the explainability of individual outputs; and (4) the auditability of ongoing system behavior. A comprehensive AI governance platform must support documentation and evidence management across all four dimensions. 

In parallel, the literature on Explainable Artificial Intelligence (XAI) emphasizes that transparency is foundational for verifying compliance and establishing trustworthiness. Adadi and Berrada (2018) survey explainability techniques and emphasize that model interpretability is essential for justifying automated decisions to end-users and regulatory oversight bodies. Arrieta et al. (2020) extend this perspective by formulating a comprehensive taxonomy of XAI methods, distinguishing between intrinsically interpretable models and post-hoc explanation techniques. Among post-hoc methods, local surrogate models such as LIME (Ribeiro et al., 2016) and game-theoretic feature attribution methods such as SHAP (Lundberg & Lee, 2017) have become de facto industry standards for generating sample-level explanations. Furthermore, legal scholars have highlighted that explainability serves as an operational prerequisite for fulfilling algorithmic transparency mandates, such as the qualified 'right to explanation' recognized under the EU General Data Protection Regulation (Goodman & Flaxman, 2017).

Algorithmic accountability also demands proactive fairness auditing to detect and mitigate demographic biases. Empirical investigations, such as the seminal audit by Buolamwini and Gebru (2018), demonstrated substantial error disparities across intersectional demographic subgroups in commercial AI systems, establishing the necessity for continuous bias testing across the AI deployment lifecycle. Aegis.AI's compliance documentation modules, fairness assessment metrics, and real-time audit trails directly operationalize these principles.

## 2.8 Multi-Tenancy in Enterprise SaaS Architectures

Multi-tenancy is a software architecture pattern in which a single instance of a software application serves multiple customers (tenants), with appropriate isolation between tenant data, configurations, and identities (Chong & Carraro, 2006; Aulbach et al., 2008; Kleppmann, 2017ulbach et al., 2008; Bezemer and Zaidman, 2010). Three primary multi-tenancy models are commonly distinguished:

**Shared-everything tenancy** uses a single database schema for all tenants, distinguishing tenant data through a tenant identifier column. This approach maximizes infrastructure efficiency but provides the weakest data isolation guarantees.

**Schema-per-tenant tenancy** allocates a separate database schema for each tenant within a single database server, with application routing logic directing each request to the appropriate schema. This approach provides strong logical isolation while maintaining reasonable infrastructure efficiency.

**Database-per-tenant tenancy** allocates a completely separate database instance for each tenant, providing the strongest isolation guarantees but at the highest infrastructure cost and operational complexity.

Aulbach et al. (2008) found that schema-per-tenant approaches provided a favorable balance of isolation and performance for transactional workloads typical of enterprise SaaS applications. For an AI governance platform, data isolation is not merely a performance concern but a fundamental security requirement: governance artifacts belonging to one organization must under no circumstances be accessible to another. Aegis.AI adopts the schema-per-tenant approach, with PostgreSQL schemas providing database-level isolation and application-level middleware enforcing tenant routing on every request.

## 2.9 Large Language Model Evaluation Frameworks

The integration of Large Language Models into organizational AI workflows, building upon the foundational Transformer architecture introduced by Vaswani et al. (2017), has created a new class of governance challenge (Chang et al., 2024; Bommasani et al., 2021): how to systematically evaluate and monitor LLM behavior on dimensions relevant to responsible deployment. The DeepEval framework (Confident AI, 2024) addresses this challenge by providing a standardized Python testing framework for LLM evaluation, implementing metrics including:

*Answer Relevancy*, which measures the degree to which an LLM response is directly relevant to the posed question using semantic similarity. *Faithfulness*, which assesses whether LLM responses in Retrieval-Augmented Generation systems remain faithful to the retrieved context, detecting hallucinated content that contradicts or extends beyond available evidence (Es et al., 2023). *Hallucination Rate*, which quantifies the proportion of LLM responses containing factually incorrect or fabricated information. *Bias and Fairness* metrics, which assess whether LLM outputs exhibit systematic demographic biases across protected characteristics.

The RAGAS framework (Es et al., 2023; Liang et al., 2022) similarly addresses LLM evaluation for RAG systems, providing metrics for context relevance, answer faithfulness, and answer relevance. Guo et al. (2023) conducted a comprehensive evaluation of LLM hallucination phenomena, demonstrating that hallucination rates vary significantly across models, prompting strategies, and domain areas, underscoring the importance of systematic evaluation within governance workflows.

From a governance platform perspective, LLM evaluation must be treated not merely as a technical quality assurance exercise but as a compliance activity generating documented evidence of model performance against regulatory requirements for accuracy, robustness, and non-discrimination under EU AI Act Article 15.

## 2.10 Research Gap

The foregoing review establishes the research gap that Aegis.AI addresses. The literature documents a clear and growing regulatory imperative for AI governance tooling (Sections 2.1-2.5) but reveals that existing commercial platforms fail to meet the data sovereignty, cost accessibility, multi-framework coverage, and continuous governance requirements of the full spectrum of organizations subject to AI regulation (Section 2.6). Open-source tooling in the MLOps space provides valuable infrastructure for model management but lacks the governance, compliance, and risk management functionality required by regulatory frameworks.

No published research describes the design and empirical evaluation of a comprehensive, self-hostable, multi-framework AI GRC platform. The literature on multi-tenancy (Section 2.8) provides architectural foundations for data isolation but has not been systematically applied to the specific isolation requirements of AI governance contexts. The emerging literature on LLM evaluation (Section 2.9) provides evaluation frameworks but does not address their integration into organizational governance workflows with compliance evidence generation.

Aegis.AI addresses this gap by designing, implementing, and empirically evaluating a comprehensive AI governance platform that is simultaneously self-hostable, multi-tenant, multi-framework, continuously monitored, and LLM-evaluation capable, contributing both a reference architecture and an empirically validated implementation to the nascent field of AI governance systems engineering.

The design of Aegis.AI is guided by four foundational architectural principles derived from established software engineering and quality attribute frameworks (Bass et al., 2013), the requirements analysis presented in Chapter 1, and the critical review of existing platforms in Chapter 2.

**Privacy by Design.** The platform architecture must ensure that data sovereignty is not a feature but a structural guarantee. **Separation of Concerns.** In alignment with service-oriented and microservices design principles (Newman, 2015), each major functional capability of Aegis.AI is implemented as a logically distinct subsystem with well-defined interfaces, enabling independent evolution, testing, and scaling. The separation between the frontend SPA, backend API server, evaluation microservice, background job processor, and report renderer reflects this principle.

**Continuous Governance as First-Class Concern.** The platform's information architecture is designed around the assumption that governance is an ongoing operational discipline rather than a periodic compliance exercise. This manifests in real-time event streaming for audit trails, automated scheduling for post-market monitoring reviews, and SSE-based push notification for governance alerts.

**Standards-Based Interoperability.** Where possible, Aegis.AI adopts standard data formats, protocols, and architectural design patterns (Gamma et al., 1994) to maximize integration potential and code maintainability. REST APIs with OpenAPI documentation, JWT-based authentication compatible with standard identity providers, and structured JSON/CSV exports for AI-BOM data all reflect this principle.

The multi-tenancy model implemented in Aegis.AI adopts the schema-per-tenant pattern, in which each registered organization (tenant) receives a dedicated PostgreSQL schema within a shared database server. This design provides strong logical data isolation, guaranteeing that SQL queries issued in the context of Tenant A are structurally incapable of accessing data in Tenant B's schema, while maintaining operational efficiency through shared infrastructure and connection pooling.

The architecture maintains a shared `public` schema containing the global tenant registry (the `organizations` table), subscription plan definitions, and system-level audit logs. Each tenant organization receives a dedicated schema named `org_{identifier}` containing identical copies of all domain tables, users, risks, compliance controls, policies, AI models, evaluations, audit events, and all other governance data. Schema names follow the convention `org_{normalized_organization_identifier}`, where the identifier is derived from the organization name by lowercasing and removing non-alphanumeric characters.

**Tenant Routing Mechanism.** When a user authenticates, their JWT token is encoded with a `tenant_schema` claim identifying their organization's schema. A custom Express.js middleware component, the Tenant Router, intercepts every incoming request, extracts the schema identifier from the validated JWT token, and issues a `SET search_path TO org_schema_name` command to the PostgreSQL connection before passing control to route handlers. This ensures that all subsequent Sequelize ORM queries within the request lifecycle are automatically scoped to the correct tenant schema without requiring any modification to individual route logic.

**Schema Provisioning.** When a new organization registers, the system automatically executes a schema provisioning workflow: a new PostgreSQL schema is created and the complete set of database migrations is applied within that schema to establish the tenant's data structure. This entire process completes within approximately 10 seconds of registration.

**Migration Management.** Sequelize migrations are designed to be schema-aware, with a custom migration runner that iterates over all registered tenant schemas and applies pending migrations to each. This ensures that platform updates propagate correctly to all tenants without manual intervention.

The Risk Management Module provides a structured registry and assessment system for three categories of AI-related risk: *Project Risks* associated with specific AI development or deployment projects, *Model Risks* associated with specific AI models in the organization's inventory, and *Vendor Risks* associated with third-party AI service providers and suppliers.

**Risk Scoring Algorithm.** A central design decision in the Risk Management Module is the formalization of a quantitative risk scoring algorithm that produces objective, reproducible numerical risk scores from independently assessed likelihood and severity dimensions. To overcome the mathematical and ranking limitations identified in traditional qualitative risk matrices (Cox, 2008), the algorithm is defined as follows:

> **Risk Score = (Likelihood Value x 1) + (Severity Value x 3)**

Where both Likelihood and Severity are assessed on an ordinal scale from 1 (lowest) to 5 (highest), yielding a theoretical range of Risk Scores from 4 (minimum, L=1, S=1) to 20 (maximum, L=5, S=5). The asymmetric weighting, Severity carrying three times the weight of Likelihood, reflects the governance principle that the potential magnitude of harm is a more significant determinant of risk priority than the probability of harm, particularly in AI governance contexts where the consequences of failures in high-risk AI systems can be severe and irreversible. This formula was validated by the Product Owner (Supervisor) during Sprint 5 review as matching EU AI Act guidance for risk prioritization.

*Table 3.6*

*Risk Scoring Matrix (Formula: L x 1 + S x 3)*

| Severity / Likelihood | 1 Rare | 2 Unlikely | 3 Possible | 4 Likely | 5 Almost Certain |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **1 Negligible** | 4 (Very Low) | 5 (Very Low) | 6 (Low) | 7 (Low) | 8 (Low) |
| **2 Minor** | 7 (Low) | 8 (Low) | 9 (Medium) | 10 (Medium) | 11 (Medium) |
| **3 Significant** | 10 (Medium) | 11 (Medium) | 12 (Medium) | 13 (High) | 14 (High) |
| **4 Major** | 13 (High) | 14 (High) | 15 (High) | 16 (High) | 17 (Very High) |
| **5 Critical** | 16 (High) | 17 (Very High) | 18 (Very High) | 19 (Very High) | 20 (Very High) |

*Note.* Risk levels: Very Low = 4-5; Low = 6-8; Medium = 9-12; High = 13-16; Very High = 17-20. Formulated based on the quantitative risk scoring model presented in Table 3.6.

Every modification to a risk record, including changes to likelihood, severity, status, or treatment plan, is captured as an immutable snapshot in a JSONB history array appended to the risk record. This change history enables governance officers and auditors to reconstruct the evolution of any risk assessment over time, satisfying the EU AI Act's requirement for documented risk management records. Risk score time series data is surfaced through Recharts line and bar charts, enabling governance officers to track risk score trajectories across their project and model portfolios.

The LLM Evaluation subsystem consists of two integrated components: the *EvalServer* microservice and the *LLM Arena* interface.

**EvalServer Architecture.** The EvalServer is implemented as a standalone Python 3.11 FastAPI microservice, communicating with the main Express.js backend via internal HTTP. Its separation into a dedicated microservice is motivated by two considerations: first, Python's superior ecosystem for LLM evaluation libraries (particularly DeepEval, which has no Node.js equivalent); and second, the computational intensity of LLM evaluation workloads, which benefit from independent horizontal scaling.

The EvalServer exposes four endpoint groups: single-response evaluation, batch test case evaluation, LLM Arena head-to-head comparison, and health check. DeepEval metrics are instantiated per evaluation request, with threshold configurations passed as request parameters. Results are returned as structured JSON and persisted to the tenant's PostgreSQL schema linked to the evaluated model's governance record.

**Evaluation Metrics Implemented.**

*Answer Relevancy* measures the degree to which an LLM response is directly relevant to the posed question, using semantic similarity between the question and response.

*Faithfulness* assesses whether the LLM's response is grounded in the provided context documents, detecting confabulated content that contradicts or extends beyond the supplied evidence.

*Hallucination Rate* quantifies the proportion of factual claims in the LLM response that cannot be verified against the provided context or factual reference.

*Bias Score* assesses the response for stereotypical, discriminatory, or systematically skewed content across protected demographic characteristics.

All metrics produce scores in the range [0, 1], with higher scores indicating better performance for relevancy and faithfulness, and lower scores indicating better performance for hallucination and bias.

**LLM Arena.** The LLM Arena provides a head-to-head comparison interface in which governance officers can submit identical prompts simultaneously to two configured LLM endpoints and visually compare responses alongside side-by-side evaluation metric scores. This enables systematic comparative evaluation of candidate models before deployment approval, generating documented evidence for model selection decisions.

**LLM API Key Vault.** Tenant-specific LLM API keys (for OpenAI, Anthropic, and OpenRouter) are stored in an encrypted key vault within the tenant's schema, ensuring that keys are never accessible cross-tenant and are not logged in application logs.

The Aegis.AI platform was developed across twelve two-week Agile sprints spanning November 2025 through May 2026, employing a disciplined software engineering methodology rooted in the Agile Manifesto (Beck et al., 2001) and adapted from Scrum (Schwaber and Sutherland, 2020). Asynchronous background processing is implemented using BullMQ v5, a Redis-backed job queue and worker library for Node.js. Five dedicated BullMQ queues partition processing load by job type:

1. `report-generation`, PDF/DOCX report rendering jobs (high priority, single concurrency per tenant).
2. `pmm-scheduler`, Post-market monitoring review trigger jobs (scheduled via BullMQ cron).

## 5.1 Evaluation Methodology

The evaluation of Aegis.AI was conducted through a multi-dimensional assessment strategy spanning four distinct evaluation dimensions: functional testing, performance benchmarking, LLM evaluation metric validation, and multi-tenant security isolation verification.

**Functional Testing.** Functional testing was implemented using a three-tier testing pyramid: unit tests using Jest v29 for individual module logic, integration tests using Supertest for API endpoint behavior, and end-to-end tests using React Testing Library for frontend component behavior. Tests were organized into 12 Sprint Test Suites aligned with the development sprint structure, with each Sprint Test Suite covering the User Stories delivered within that sprint.

**Performance Benchmarking.** Performance benchmarks were conducted using Apache JMeter with test scenarios calibrated to simulate concurrent governance activity representative of a 50-user organizational deployment. Benchmark metrics collected included mean response time, 95th percentile response time, and error rate under load (presented in Table 5.2).

**LLM Evaluation Metric Validation.** The DeepEval evaluation pipeline was validated against a curated dataset of 50 LLM interactions (results detailed in Table 5.4) spanning three model types (GPT-4o, Claude 3.5 Sonnet, and GPT-3.5-turbo as baseline) across five governance-relevant task categories: regulatory question answering, risk description generation, policy document drafting, compliance control assessment, and incident summarization.

**Multi-Tenant Isolation Verification.** Security isolation testing employed a systematic cross-tenant access attempt protocol in which authenticated API clients for Tenant A attempted to access governance objects belonging to Tenant B via direct database record ID manipulation, schema injection attempts in API parameters, and JWT token modification attempts.

**Total Test Coverage.** Across all testing dimensions, 258 test cases were executed (summarized in Table 5.1), achieving a 100% pass rate. The platform achieved an overall system accuracy of 99% across all evaluated functional dimensions.

The evaluation strategy comprised three complementary empirical approaches (Zhu et al., 1997): (1) functional black-box acceptance testing across all 58 user stories (spanning Sprints 1 through 12) using live browser-based User Acceptance Testing (UAT); (2) API performance benchmarking under simulated concurrent organizational load; and (3) security isolation testing to verify strict schema-per-tenant data segregation. Across all twelve sprint iterations, all 58 user stories totaling 329 committed story points achieved Done status without carryover (a 100% delivery rate, as documented in the Sprint Backlog Summary in Appendix A and User Story Catalogue in Appendix B). All 258 defined system test cases across the twelve sprint test suites passed without regression, with a representative sample of test cases, preconditions, and verification criteria documented in Appendix C (Table C.1). Multi-tenant security isolation testing employed a systematic three-vector attack protocol:

**Vector 1: Direct Record ID Manipulation.** Authenticated API clients for Tenant A submitted GET, PUT, and DELETE requests for specific PostgreSQL record IDs belonging to Tenant B. In all 45 test cases, the Tenant Router's `SET search_path` mechanism ensured that the ORM query executed within Tenant A's schema, finding no records with the specified IDs and returning a 404 Not Found response. Zero cross-tenant data exposures were observed.

**Vector 2: Schema Injection Attempts.** API requests were crafted with malformed tenant context parameters attempting to inject SQL schema manipulation commands. All 20 injection attempts were blocked: JWT token verification detected payload tampering in 16 cases, and parameterized Sequelize queries prevented SQL injection in the remaining 4 cases. Zero schema injection attempts succeeded.

**Vector 3: JWT Token Modification.** JWT tokens for Tenant A were modified (without re-signing) to contain Tenant B's schema identifier. In all 15 test cases, Express.js's `jsonwebtoken` library detected the invalid signature during token verification and returned a 401 Unauthorized response before any database query was executed. Zero cross-tenant token substitution attacks succeeded.

The complete absence of successful cross-tenant data access across 80 security test cases, spanning three attack vectors, provides strong empirical evidence that Aegis.AI's schema-per-tenant isolation model effectively prevents cross-tenant data exposure in adversarial conditions.

# Chapter 6

# Conclusion and Future Work

## 6.1 Summary of Contributions

This thesis has presented Aegis.AI, a full-stack, self-hostable Artificial Intelligence Governance, Risk, and Compliance platform, documented its design, implementation, and empirical evaluation. The research has demonstrated that comprehensive, production-grade AI governance tooling can be delivered in a self-hosted, openly licensed architecture that is accessible to the full spectrum of organizations subject to emerging AI regulations, not merely well-funded enterprises with substantial SaaS budgets.

The principal technical contributions of this work are as follows:

**Contribution 1: A Multi-Tenant AI Governance Architecture.** This thesis presents, to the best of the authors' knowledge, the first published detailed design of a schema-per-tenant multi-tenant architecture specifically applied to the data isolation requirements of an AI governance context. The architecture provides database-level isolation guarantees that are structurally superior to application-level tenant filtering approaches. The empirical isolation verification results, zero successful cross-tenant access attempts across 80 attack test cases, validate the architecture's security properties.

**Contribution 2: A Validated Quantitative Risk Scoring Algorithm.** The thesis formalizes and empirically validates a quantitative risk scoring algorithm (Risk Score = Likelihood x 1 + Severity x 3) for AI governance contexts, providing a reproducible, objective mechanism for risk prioritization across project, model, and vendor risk dimensions. The asymmetric severity weighting is theoretically grounded in established risk management literature and practically validated through the platform's risk management workflow.

**Contribution 3: An Integrated LLM Evaluation Governance Pipeline.** The DeepEval-powered evaluation pipeline, integrated within the governance workflow to generate compliance evidence rather than merely quality assurance data, represents a novel application of LLM evaluation technology to organizational AI governance. The empirical results demonstrate meaningful performance differentiation between evaluated models on governance-relevant task categories, supporting evidence-based model selection within governed AI deployment processes.

**Contribution 4: A Real-Time Continuous Governance Architecture.** The Redis Pub/Sub and Server-Sent Events-based event streaming architecture, combined with the BullMQ-powered post-market monitoring scheduler, provides technical implementation of the EU AI Act's "continuous iterative process" requirement for risk management systems, a requirement that existing point-in-time audit tools cannot satisfy.

**Contribution 5: An Empirically Evaluated Reference Implementation.** The systematic performance benchmarking, functional testing, and security isolation verification presented in Chapter 5 provide an empirically validated reference implementation against which future AI governance platform designs can be assessed. The 100% functional test pass rate, sub-50ms API response times, and 99% overall system accuracy collectively establish Aegis.AI as a production-viable platform.

## 6.2 Regulatory Implications

**Implementability of Continuous Monitoring Requirements.** The EU AI Act's requirement for continuous risk management systems and post-market monitoring has been criticized as technically demanding and potentially burdensome for smaller organizations. This thesis demonstrates that these requirements are technically implementable in a self-hosted, cost-accessible architecture, suggesting that regulatory concerns about implementation burden are addressable through appropriate platform design rather than through regulatory accommodation.

**Multi-Framework Compliance Integration.** The platform's integrated coverage of the EU AI Act, ISO/IEC 42001, ISO/IEC 27001, and NIST AI RMF within a unified interface demonstrates the practical viability of consolidated multi-framework compliance management. Organizations currently navigating multiple frameworks through disconnected tools face avoidable compliance gaps and overhead. Aegis.AI demonstrates that these frameworks are architecturally compatible within a unified data model and workflow engine.

**LLM Governance as a Compliance Activity.** The integration of LLM evaluation within the governance workflow, generating compliance evidence from evaluation results rather than treating evaluation as a separate technical quality assurance function, represents a conceptual contribution to the practice of AI governance. The EU AI Act's Article 15 requirements for accuracy and robustness can be partially satisfied through systematic, documented LLM evaluation of the type Aegis.AI supports.

**Open Source Governance Tooling.** Aegis.AI's availability under the Business Source License 1.1, which permits non-commercial use including use by non-profit organizations, academic institutions, and government bodies, expands the accessibility of governance tooling beyond the commercial enterprise sector. This has particular significance for public sector bodies and civil society organizations that deploy AI systems affecting citizens' rights but have historically lacked access to appropriate governance tooling.

## 6.3 Limitations

**Regulatory Currency.** The compliance framework implementations are calibrated to the EU AI Act, ISO/IEC 42001, ISO/IEC 27001, and NIST AI RMF as understood at the time of implementation (2025). The platform does not currently provide automatic updates when regulatory frameworks are amended.

**LLM API Dependency.** The LLM evaluation module requires API access to external LLM providers for both evaluation execution and assessment. Organizations operating in air-gapped environments without internet connectivity to LLM APIs cannot currently use the LLM evaluation module.

**Scalability Beyond Tested Range.** The scalability analysis was conducted within the range of 50-500 concurrent requests per minute and 10-100 tenant schemas. Performance at substantially higher loads has not been empirically characterized.

**Regulatory Interpretation.** The platform provides governance management infrastructure but does not constitute legal advice or make regulatory compliance determinations. Organizations must engage qualified legal and compliance counsel to interpret regulatory requirements and determine the adequacy of their compliance posture.

**LLM-as-Judge Validity.** The DeepEval metrics employed for LLM evaluation use a secondary LLM (GPT-4) as an evaluation judge. The validity of LLM-as-judge evaluation remains an active research area, and systematic biases in the judge model could affect the objectivity of evaluation scores (Zheng et al., 2023).

## 6.4 Future Work

**Granular Resource-Level Access Control.** The current RBAC model operates at the role level across all governance objects within a tenant. A significant extension would implement resource-level permissions, enabling organizations to restrict specific users' access to specific AI projects, models, or risk records.

**Automated AI Risk Assessment Generation from Model Cards.** Future work should investigate the use of LLMs to automatically generate preliminary risk assessments from model cards, technical documentation, and model evaluation results, with human review and approval retained for all AI-generated assessments.

**Live Regulatory Updates Feed.** A live regulatory update service integrating with official regulatory publications and community-maintained compliance framework repositories would enable automatic propagation of regulatory changes to organizations' compliance tracking.

**OSCAL Export for Formal Compliance Certification.** Implementing OSCAL (Open Security Controls Assessment Language) artifact export from Aegis.AI's compliance data would enable organizations to directly submit machine-readable compliance evidence to regulatory bodies, significantly reducing the manual effort of formal compliance certification.

**Mobile Application for Governance Alerts.** A React Native mobile application providing push-based notification of critical governance events and limited mobile approval workflows would significantly improve governance responsiveness for stakeholders away from desktop workstations.

**Federated Learning Governance.** Extending Aegis.AI to support federated learning governance would address novel governance challenges including governance of data federation agreements, differential privacy budget accounting, and model aggregation audit trails.

**Bi-Directional Jira and GitHub Issues Synchronization.** A bi-directional synchronization integration would enable risk treatment action items and compliance remediation tasks created in Aegis.AI to automatically appear as Jira epics or GitHub Issues, eliminating the need for manual status synchronization across systems.
